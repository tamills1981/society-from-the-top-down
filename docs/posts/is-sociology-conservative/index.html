Is Sociology Conservative?

Some Thoughts on Shamus Khan’s British Journal of Sociology Lecture

sociology
conservatism
Published

November 9, 2026

Last week I dropped by the LSE on my way through London to catch Shamus Khan’s British Journal of Sociology lecture, ‘The conservative tradition in sociological thought’. Glad I did. It was a thought-provoking talk.

Khan, for those who don’t know, is professor of sociology at Princeton. He writes on elites and inequality and his first book, Privilege, was a celebrated study of an American private school. So very much up my street. He covered a lot of ground, and it’s difficult to do justice to an hour-long lecture from memory, especially when you didn’t take notes. Gather they’ll be a published version at some point with Craig Calhoun, so really any serious critical engagement should be put on hold till then. For now, I’ll just try and briefly sketch out what I remember and what I made of it.

The lecture grew out of a course Khan teaches on conservatism. The overall argument was that sociology owes a largely unacknowledged debt to conservative thought. He outlined several areas of sociology he suggested had been influenced by conservatism. These included a focus on the family and community, a rejection of individualism, and an emphasis on bonds of mutual dependency. He argued, for example, that sociological conceptions of freedom have some affinity with conservative thought since – in contrast to the liberal conception of freedom as the absence of constraint – for sociologists, individuals are understood as living in networks of social obligations. Relationships, e g. friendships or marriages, are understood not as constraints on individual freedom, but as institutions that give our lives meaning.

He also spent some time discussing the conservative idea of legitimate hierarchy, noting that sociologists have resisted addressing the question of what sorts of hierarchies or inequalities might be legitimate. He gave the examples here of the different treatment of minors and the different sets of rights afforded to citizens and non-citizens of nation states. He focused particularly on the sociological critique of meritocracy, pointing out that sociologists have generally focused on how societies have in practice fallen short of meritocratic ideals, but have largely not critiqued the underlying principle – that is the allocation of resources based on individual merit, however that is conceived (talent, hard work etc). He was going to discuss elites specifically, which would have been interesting, but ran short of time.

As I said, all quite thought provoking. But the broad argument didn’t sit right with me. For Khan, the sociological vision of society as comprising not isolated individuals, but a complex web of relations, obligations, symbolic meanings and distributed knowledge, has an affinity with conservative thought. But I didn’t go away with the sense that this is so much an intellectual debt of sociology to conservatism, as a historical product of the emergence and evolution of the two ‘thought styles’.

I agree there is a conservative tradition within sociology. There’s a case for considering Durkheim’s sociology (if not his politics) as conservative. Comte even more so. And the less said about Herbert Spencer the better. I think it’s also true that what in sociology textbooks gets called functionalism was basically conservative for the same reasons. These approaches tend towards seeing society as an organic whole or system which enables moral and social order under conditions of complexity. Conflict and disruption are bad.

Khan started his lecture by drawing on Mannheim’s famous essay on conservatism, which distinguishes it from traditionalism. The key point here is that conservatism is not merely a temperamental attachment to tradition, but represents the political mobilisation of such sentiments in response to the challenge posed to traditional authority by the French Revolution, liberalism more generally, and later socialism (‘modernity’ if you like).

Khan’s use of Mannheim would seem to suggest understanding conservativism as a distinctly political intellectual tradition. Not in the narrow sense of the term, i.e. relating to the state and the political sphere, but political with a ‘small p’. Yet the lecture seemed to me too idealist. Its not enough to note the similarity of particular themes and preoccupations without putting the development and mobilisation of these thought styles in historical and political context.

Conservatism is an ideas based praxis reacting to the rationalist interrogation of social hierarchy. In terms of the affinity with sociology, it is the relationship with liberalism that is key. The classic canonical texts of sociology all in their own way grapple with the question of modernity from the perspective of Europeans – capitalism, bureaucracy, urbanisation and secularisation etc. They all have an uneasy relationship with these dynamics; even Weber. This is what drives the affinity Khan describes, and on this I think we agree.

As it evolved as a distinct discipline, sociology maintained this unease, whilst carving out a niche alongside economics, which in contrast dutifully reified the dominant ideology of capitalism, assuming much greater prestige and influence as a result. Sociology, meanwhile, was either relegated to a position of critique, or left to analyse the space left behind by the ideologues of the market (community, the family, culture, status, class, occupations etc). This naturally meant some resonances with conservatism. But they are shallow. Sociology has not been conservative politically (Durkheim himself was not) and I don’t think this fact should be put aside analytically.

For sure, there have been some conservative sociologists in its more mature phase. We could name some names. Robert Nisbet is the standout example, who Khan mentioned. In the UK there’s Frank Furedi of course, who leads the Living Marxism cult, and I mentioned Peter Saunders in a previous post. But these are very much the exception. Even if Durkheim has had an enduring influence on the discipline, I don’t see much left of his preoccupation with moral regulation and social order today. And who reads Talcott Parsons these days?

There’s a good reason why sociology has so often been on the receiving end of reactionary attacks on scholarship. It is – or at least has aspired to be – a science of society. It’s not that it is necessarily left-wing. But if it is scientific and rigorous it should puncture the fantasies of both conservativism and liberalism. And even if relegated to the margins beyond the gaze of the theologians of capital, it still offers theories, methods and a body of knowledge that sits uneasily with dominant ideas. These may offer some intellectual resources for anti-liberal reaction. But conservatism is a scavenger ideology defined by political expediency not consistent ideological commitments. On that point, I will give the last word to Karl Marx:

…the Tories in England long imagined that they were enthusiastic about monarchy, the church, and the beauties of the old English Constitution, until the day of danger wrung from them the confession that they are enthusiastic only about ground rent.
